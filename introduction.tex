\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cha:intro}

Even though most of the common graph theory notions and objects are well
understood, the area lacks a consistent and unified terminology. For that
matter, we devote this chapter to the introduction of the basic definitions
along with notation. We also present some ground results on which we build our
work. Even for the familiarized reader, we encourage to at least skim through
the sections, as we introduce some non-standard notations for operations between
graphs, as well as conventions for certain types of partitions of graphs. All
definitions not considered in here can be found in either \cite{bondy2008} or
\cite{diestel2017}.

\section{Graphs and algorithms}
\label{sec:graphs}

All graphs considered in this work are finite, undirected with neither loops nor
parallel edges. We denote by $V$ and $E$ the vertex and edge sets of a graph
$G$, respectively. For a vertex $v\in V$, $N(v)$ is the set of neighbors of $v$,
and the degree of a vertex is $d(v) = |N(v)|$. For $S\subseteq V$, we write
$G[S]$ for the subgraph of $G$ induced by $S$. When two graphs $G$ and $H$ are
isomorphic, we write $G \cong H$.

The complement of a graph $G$ is denoted by $\overline{G}$, and the disjoint
union of two graphs $G$ and $H$ is referred to as $G + H$. For a positive
integer $k$, denote by $kG$ the disjoint union of $k$ copies of $G$. We denote
by $G-S$ the subgraph $G[V\setminus S]$ --- if $S$ is a singleton, then we write
$G-v$ instead of $G-\l{v}$.

For a positive integer $n$, we denote by $K_n$, $C_n$ and $P_n$ the complete
graph, the cycle, and the path on $n$ vertices, respectively. We say that a set
$S\subseteq V$ is independent or stable if $G[S] \cong \overline{K_{|S|}}$, and
$S$ is a clique if $G[S] \cong K_{|S|}$. We say that a graph is a cluster if it
is the disjoint union of complete graphs, and a graph is a complete multipartite
if it is the complement of a cluster. We call a graph $G$ chordal if it does not
contain any induced cycle of length at least four, that is, if $C$ is a cycle in
$G$ of length at least four, then there is a pair of adjacent non-consecutive
vertices of $C$; such an edge is called a chord of $C$. If the vertex set of
graph $G$ can be partitioned into $(S,K)$ such that $S$ is stable and $K$ is a
clique, then we call $G$ a split graph. Every time we work on a split graph, we
provide partitions as in the format $(S,K)$, and while we sometimes use variants
like $(S',K')$ or $(S_1, K_1)$,  the $S$ part  always indicate the stable one,
while $K$ always indicate the clique one.

We say that a vertex $v$ is universal if it is adjacent to all other vertices of
$G$, and isolated if it is adjacent to none. Additionally, $v$ is said to be
simplicial if $N(v)$ is a clique. Given $W_1$ and $W_2$ disjoint subsets of $V$,
we say that $W_1$ is complete to $W_2$ if all vertices in $W_1$ are adjacent to
all vertices in $W_2$. Similarly, if all vertices in $W_1$ are not adjacent to
all vertices in $W_2$, we say that $W_1$ is anticomplete to $W_2$. Sometimes,
rather than talking about completeness or anticompleteness between sets, it is
easier to identify a set by the graph it induces. Hence, we often interchange
the sets $W_1$ and $W_2$ with the graphs $G[W_1]$ and $G[W_2]$, so when we say
that $G[W_1]$ is complete to $G[W_2]$, we mean it as in $W_1$ being complete to
$W_2$.

We say that a graph is a hole if it is a cycle of length at least five, and a
graph is an anti-hole if its complement is a hole. A graph is an odd hole if it
is a hole of odd length, and similarly we define an odd anti-hole. For a set of
graphs $\mathcal{G}$, we say that a graph $H$ is $\mathcal{G}$-free when $H$
does not contain, as an induced subgraph, any of the graphs in $\mathcal{G}$. We
usually enumerate the members of $\mathcal{G}$ as a tuple instead of a set, and
sometimes completely avoid curly brackets or parentheses when $\mathcal{G}$
consists of a single graph.   Notice that cluster graphs are exactly the
$P_3$-free graphs, while complete multipartite graphs are the $(K_1 + K_2)$-free
graphs. Finally, in \Cref{fig:classic} we present two classic graphs that we
frequently refer to (diamond and house), along with other graphs that we just
refer to in the conclusions.

Now we briefly discuss some notions regarding algorithms on graphs. Mainly, we
are interested in the running time of algorithms. For that matter, we use the
standard big-$O$ notation to describe asymptotic behavior. For all algorithms
described in this work, whenever we say that it runs in linear time, we mean
that its time complexity is $O(|V| + |E|)$.

It is well known that for an input graph $G$, it is possible to order its
adjacency lists to have compatible orderings, i.e., there is a linear ordering
of $V$ such that within each adjacency list, the vertices appear in that
ordering.   Therefore, we assume that this is always the case.

Given a partition $\mathcal{P}$ of constant size for an input graph $G$, it is
possible to construct copies of the subgraphs $G[P]$ for each $P \in
\mathcal{P}$ in linear time by traversing the adjacency lists of each vertex in
$G$.   Thus, given an input graph $G$, it is possible to execute a linear-time
algorithm in each $G[P]$ in time proportional to the number of vertices and
edges of $G[P]$.   It is of particular interest for this work to know that
$G[P]$ can be tested to be empty, complete, split or a cluster graph in linear
time. Checking whether a graph is either empty or complete is easily
accomplished by traversing the adjacency lists of each vertex, verifying that
each list is either empty or contains all vertices, and getting either a pair of
adjacent or nonadjacent vertices as a no-certificate for emptiness or
completeness, respectively (of course, this can also be done by looking at the
length of the adjacency list, but the described strategy also works for
verifying whether a subset is a clique or an independent set). Meanwhile, to
check whether a graph is a cluster, we can run a BFS to find all connected
components, and then verify that the degree of each vertex is equal to the size
of its component minus one; otherwise, a second pass of BFS can be executed to
find an induced copy of $P_3$. The case of split graphs is discussed in
\Cref{sec:chordal-defs}.

\section{Exact neighborhoods}
\label{sec:exact-neigh}

When deciding if a graph $G$ can be partitioned in a particular way, it is often
useful to know how vertices of $G$ behave with respect to a subgraph $H$ of $G$.
One of the earliest examples of this is due to Brandst\"adt in
\cite{brandstadt1996}, where the author determines when a graph $G$ has a
partition generalizing that of a split graph. This is done by fixing a partial
partition on the vertices of the graph depending on whether their neighborhoods
have a desired structure. Afterwards, such a partial partition is used to decide
if it can be extended to a whole partition, once more analyzing the
neighborhoods of the remaining vertices. For our purposes, we intend to use a
refined version of the aforementioned technique, now introduced with a proper
name and a rather easy description.

Given a subgraph $H$ of $G$ with vertex set $W$, we want to classify the
vertices of $G-W$ based on which vertices of $H$ they are adjacent to. Thus, for
each $S\subseteq W$, let $N^W_S$ (or $N_S$ when there is no ambiguity) be the
set of vertices $x$ of $G-W$ such that $N(x)\cap W = S$. From the definition it
follows that the vertices in $N_S$ are those outside $H$ that are adjacent to
exactly the vertices in $S$ and to no other vertex of $H$. Hence, the sets $N_S$
receive a convenient name: exact neighborhoods. It is immediate that sets $N_S$,
along with $W$, partition the set $V$.

Suppose now that $H$ is not a subgraph of graph $G$, but it is a fixed graph. If
we happen to know that $G$ contains a subgraph isomorphic to $H$, $G'$, then we
can partition the vertices of $G$ as stated in the last paragraph. Moreover,
since $H$ is fixed, the partiton obtained is of constant size. This yields to a
fairly simple algorithm that calculates the exact neighborhoods in linear time
provided that $G'$ is labeled: for each vertex $x$ not in $G'$, scan its
adjacency list to decide which vertices of $G'$ are adjacent to $x$. This
determines a label that gets assigned to $x$ indicating the exact neighborhood
it belongs to.

The advantages of the partition go beyond the classification of vertices. Given
a pair of distinct exact neighborhoods $N_X$ and $N_Y$, we can decide in linear
time if two of these are complete or anticomplete to each other. Indeed, it
suffices to go through the adjacency list of each vertex in $N_X$ and verify
that each has as many neighbors in $N_Y$ as $|N_Y|$. If not, then at some point
the algorithm finds a vertex $x$ in $N_X$ such that it is not adjacent to all
vertices in $N_Y$, and scanning the adjacency lists of the vertices in $N_Y$
will eventually yield a vertex $y\in N_Y$ not adjacent to $x$. Likewise, to
prove that $N_X$ is anticomplete to $N_Y$, we verify for each vertex in $N_X$
that the number of neighbors it has in $N_Y$ is zero, otherwise, one immediately
gets two adjacent vertices witnessing the no-anticompleteness.

\section{Hereditary properties}
\label{sec:hereditary}

Among the several ways to classify graphs, one of great use is by looking at the
properties they satisfy. We conceptualize this idea by straight up calling a
family of graphs a property, this with the intention of the graphs in each
family to indeed satisfy a fixed set of properties --- hence, we indistinctly
use class, familiy and property with the same meaning. We distinguish when a
class of graphs is broad enough to the point that it is closed under taking
induced subgraphs of it elements. This is what we call a hereditary property,
conveniently named to reflect that the property that defines the class is passed
on to all induced subgraphs.

The importance of hereditary properties resides on a useful charcterization of
them. From the definition it follows that $K_1$ is in any nonempty hereditary
class of graphs $\mathcal{F}$. Thus, for any $G\notin \mathcal{F}$, there must
be an induced subgraph $H$ of $G$ such that $H\notin \mathcal{F}$ and it is
minimal, that is, $H-x$ is in $\mathcal{F}$ for any vertex $x$ of $H$. Graph $H$
is called a minimal obstruction for $\mathcal{F}$ or that $H$ is an minimal
$\mathcal{F}$ obstruction. Let $\text{Obs}(\mathcal{F})$ be the set of all
minimal obstructions for the class $\mathcal{F}$. It is obvious that a graph $G$
is in $\mathcal{F}$ if and only if $G$ is $\text{Obs}(\mathcal{F})$-free. This
implies that either one can design a hereditary property to consist of the
graphs free of the $G$'s in a fixed class $\mathcal{F}$, or one can start with
an hereditary property and then determine which are its minimal obstructions. As
an example of the first idea, we concentrate our work on the class of
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free graphs.

Notice that the class of $(P_5,\text{house})$-free graphs satisfies an
additional condition than just being hereditary. Notice that
$\overline{P_5}=\text{house}$. Therefore, if graph $G$ is
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free, then we additionally know that $\overline{G}$ is also
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free. This extends to general hereditary classes
$\mathcal{F}$ when the set of minimal obstructions is closed under
complementation, which implies that $\mathcal{F}$ is also closed under
complementation --- it is easy to see that this is an if and only if. The
decision problem of deciding whether a graph belongs to $\mathcal{F}$ is called
the $\mathcal{F}$-recognition problem.

Another relevant type of class is that of additive properties. A property is
additive if it is closed under taking disjoint unions. From here, it is clear
that the minimal obstructions for a hereditary and additive property
$\mathcal{F}$ are connected.

To end the section, we take a look at an important result concerning this type
of properties. In general, the most interesting partition problems in the area
arise by combining (or composing) several types of hereditary properties. For
example, the property of being and empty graph is hereditary, and so we get to
the problem of deciding when a graph $G$ admits a partition of its vertex set
into $(V_1,V_2)$ such that both $G[V_1]$ and $G[V_2]$ are empty graphs --- this
is none other than the problem of deciding when $G$ is bipartite, and it is
clear that the family of bipartite graphs is hereditary. The last construction
can be generalized as follows: Take a positive integer $k$ and $k$ fixed
hereditary classes of graphs, $\mathcal{F}_1, \dots, \mathcal{F}_k$. If we allow
graphs to have partitions in which some parts may be empty, then we get that the
class of graphs $G$ admiting a vertex partition into $k$ sets $(V_1,\dots,V_k)$
such that $G[V_i]\in \mathcal{F}_i$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$, is a
hereditary property.

Regarding these kinds of hereditary properties, the following result by Farrugia
\cite{farrugia2004} tells us that one can only do so much in terms of the time
complexity to recognize these families.

\begin{theorem}[\cite{farrugia2004}]
\label{thm:farrugia}
    Let $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$ be additive hereditary properties
    with at least one of them not being the class of empty graphs. Then,
    recognizing the class of graphs $G$ with a vertex partition $(V_1,V_2)$ such
    that $G[V_1]\in \mathcal{F}_1$ and $G[V_2]\in \mathcal{F}_2$ is NP-hard.
    Furthermore, recognizing such a class is NP-complete if and only if both
    $\mathcal{F}_1$-recognition and $\mathcal{F}_2$-recognition is in NP.
\end{theorem}

We use this theorem in \Cref{sec:polarity-defs} to establish some initial
time-complexity results of the problem we are going to tackle.

\section{Chordal graphs and its subclasses}
\label{sec:chordal-defs}

A well-studied class of graphs is that of chordal graphs defined in
\Cref{sec:graphs}. From the definition it follows that this class is hereditary,
addtive and its minimal obstructions are all cycles of length at least $4$.
These graphs have been widely studied under different names, and their
distinguished applications and properties led to the need to recognize such a
family in an efficient way. In \cite{fulkerson1965}, Fulkerson and Gross
proposed a condition that all chordal graphs must satisfy, and such a conditions
can be verified in linear time. Here, we develop that same idea as Golumbic
presented it in \cite{golumbic2004}.

A perfect elimination ordering for a graph $G$ is a linear ordering of its
vertices $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ such that, for each $i$, $v_i$ is a simplicial vertex
of $G[\l{v_i,\dots, v_n}]$, i.e., the forward neighborhood of vertex $v_i$ is a
clique. In \cite{golumbic2004}, it is argued a graph is chordal if and only if
it has a perfect elimination ordering of its vertices. Moreover, it is shown
that such an ordering can be obtained in linear time by means of a Lex-BFS,
outputting the ordering as a yes-certificate.

The linear-time implementation of Lex-BFS is due to Rose, Tarjan and Lueker in
\cite{lexbfs}. Later, in \cite{chordal-recog} and \cite{chordal-recog-addendum},
Tarjan and Yannakakis present a similar algorithm that not only finds the
ordering if it exists, but it is able to return an induced cycle of length at
least $4$ as a no-certificate if the graph is not chordal. Furthermore, every
maximal clique in a chordal graph is such that it consists of a vertex $x$ and
its forward neighborhood in a perfect elimination ordering of the graph. Hence,
finding a clique of maximum size is achievable by calculating the cardinalities
of the forward neighborhoods and picking whichever attains the maximum.
Throughout our work, we use the fact that there is a linear-time certifying
algorithm that decides if a graph is chordal and that we can find a maximum
clique within the same time bound.

There is a particular class of chordal graphs that is of special interest.
Recall that split graphs are those whose vertex set can be partitioned into
$(S,K)$, with $S$ a stable set and $K$ a clique. At first sight, it does not
seem that split graphs are indeed chordal. However, in an unavailable work of
F\"oldes and Hammer, published as proceedings, authors point out that split
graphs are exactly the $\p{2K_2,C_4,C_5}$-free graphs, which in turn imply that
they are free of any induced cycle of length at least 4 noting that every $C_k$
with $k\geq 6$ contains an induced copy of $2K_2$. Later in \cite{hammer1981},
Hammer and Simeone found a necessary and suficient algebraic condition on the
degree sequence of a graph $G$ for it to be split. Thus, this implied the
existence of a linear-time algorithm that recognize split graphs, although it
lacked no-certificates and the yes-certificate was an equation instead of a
proper split partition. Nonetheless, in \cite{heggernesNJC14,hellDAM141} authors
devise linear-time certyfing algorithms that, in this case, either returns a
split partition, or a minimal obstruction contained as an induced subgraph in
the input graph.

As with chordal graphs, if the graph is split, the algorithm can be complemented
with a fast check on the vertices of the clique part for it to be a maximum
clique. Once more, we frequently invoke this algorithm to find either
obstructions or split partitions. Additionally, a quick check on the adjacency
lists can verify if the graph is empty. In those case, notice that we can make
the $K$ part to be empty. Otherwise, if the graph is not empty, then $K$ is not
only nonempty, but making it a maximum clique leads to it being nontrivial. It
follows that there is a certifying algorithm that recognizes split graphs and,
if it is split, it returns a partition $(S,K)$ with $K$ either empty or a
nontrivial maximal clique.

\section{Polarity, monopolarity and previous results}
\label{sec:polarity-defs}

Continuing with the examples of hereditary properties, one that also has been
widely studied is the class of polar graphs, although these have received many
names. Polar graphs were first described in \cite{tyshkevich1985} as those
graphs $G$ such that their vertex set admits a partition $(A,B)$ with the
property that $G[A]$ is a complete multipartite graph, while $G[B]$ is a cluster
graph. It is easy to see that polar graphs generalize several well-known
families of graphs, mainly bipartite and split graphs. Another trivial fact
about polar graphs is that they form a hereditary class. Nonetheless, several
hard problems (in general) which have been proved to be efficiently solvable in
bipartite and split graphs, tend to remain difficult when restricted to polar
graphs, like \textsc{3-Colorability} \cite{barbato2021}.

Not only solving problems on polar graphs tend to remain hard, Chernyak and
Chernyak proved in \cite{chernyak1986} that recognizing polar graphs is
NP-complete --- we write \textsc{Polarity} to denote the decision problem of
deciding whether a graph is polar. It is important to note here that, even
though cluster graphs and complete multipartite graphs have a finite number of
minimal obstructions ($P_3$ and $K_1 + K_2$, respectively), we have that there
is an infinite amount of minimal polar obstructions, even when restricted to
those that are chordal \cite{chordal}. More recently, Le and Nevries proved in
\cite{Le2014} that the problem remains NP-complete even in the class of planar
triangle-free graphs of maximum degree 3, and in the class of planar
$\p{C_4,\dots,C_k}$-free graphs of maximum degree 3 for every $k\geq 4$.

Knowing that \textsc{Polarity} stands out has a hard problem, one would like to
find an intermediate problem or property in hopes of finding the core of the
difficulty within the problem. Even though several degrees of polarity have been
proposed, the one that stands the most is monopolarity. A graph is said to be
monopolar if it has a polar partition $(A,B)$ such that $G[A]$ is an empty graph
rather than a complete multipartite graph --- similar to \textsc{Polarity}, we
denote by \textsc{Monopolarity} the corresponding decision problem. This version
of monopolar graphs is introduced in \cite{chordal} as an intermediate problem
aiming to solve \textsc{Polarity} in chordal graphs. This approach is successful
as authors were able to solve the problem in polynomial time. Similar positive
results were obtained in \cite{ekim2008}, where it is proved that
\textsc{Polarity} is polynomial-time solvable in the class of $P_4$-free graphs,
otherwise known as cographs. However, one should notice that even this problem
quite difficult: in \Cref{pro:additive} we show that the class of monopolar
graphs is additive. Thereby, \Cref{thm:farrugia} implies that
\textsc{Monopolarity} is NP-complete.

That being the case, we would like to explore the problem of polarity on new
family of graphs, one that we have the security that at least
\textsc{Monopolarity} is a traceable problem.

A good approach to decide which family is suitable for the problem is to proceed
as it has been done regarding other hard problems in graph theory. For example,
\textsc{Coloring} has been analyzed for all families of graphs that forbid
exactly one graph, also known as monogenic families. This has allowed us to
identify the structures that inherently make the problem hard. By the results in
\cite{Le2014}, \textsc{Polarity} is known to be NP-complete on almost all
monogenic families, so we decided to deep dive into a bigenic family, that is, a
class with two forbidden graphs. One natural family that has not been explored
and arises as a natural continuation of the work done in \cite{chordal,ekim2008}
is that of $(P_5,\text{house})$-free graphs, which has the additional property
of being self-complementary. Note that polar graphs are also closed under
complementation.

Currently, nothing is known about the complexity of \textsc{Polarity} in
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free graphs. Nonetheless, a positive result in
\cite{Le2014} yields to a $O(|V|^4)$-time algorithm that decides monopolarity on
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free graphs. Furthermore, even though the algorithm runs in
polynomial time, we do not know if there is a finite number of
$(P_5,\text{house})$-free minimal monopolar obstructions.

For sake of completeness and before we start with the main chapters, we prove
the following proposition and corollaries that follow from the discussion in
\Cref{sec:hereditary}.

\begin{proposition}
\label{pro:additive}
    Let $G$ and $H$ be graphs.   If $G$ and $H$ are monopolar, then $G+H$ is
    monopolar.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    Let $(A_1, B_1)$ and $(A_2, B_2)$ be the monopolar partitions of $G$ and
    $H$, respectively.   Since $A_1$ and $A_2$ are independent, we have that
    $A_1 \cup A_2$ is also independent.   Analogously, $G[B_1]$ and $G[B_2]$ are
    clusters, so their union is also a cluster.   Hence, $(A_1 \cup A_2, B_1
    \cup B_2)$ is a monopolar partition of $G+H$.
    
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
    A graph is monopolar if and only if each of its connected components is
    monopolar.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}
    Every minimal obstruction for monopolarity is a connected graph.
\end{corollary}
